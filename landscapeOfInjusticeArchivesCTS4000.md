# Landscape of Injustice Archives 

The historical database that I have chosen is the *Landscape of Injustice Archives*, created by the University of Victoria. This data base contains thousands of records about Japanese Canadian history during World War Two, and how over 22,000 Japanese Canadians were forced out of their homes. Their belongings were taken and sold without their consent, these items included land, homes and personal belongings; they were then sent to internment camps. This violent and racist work was executed in a way that made it appear legal and organized. The government had multiple items that made this forced displacement look justified such as documents and forms. This report will look at how the government went about collecting this data, what they left out as well as how it has been researched today. Data is very important for recording event, but it can also cause harm especially when power imbalances are involved. 

Most of the documents in the *Landscape of Injustice Archives* were created between 1942 and 1949 by a government called the *Custodian of Enemy Property*. Some of these documents where lists of homes, bank accounts, boats and businesses that were taken from the Japanese Canadian when they were forcibly removed. Most of these items were sold at a very low cost by the government. None of the Japanese Canadians were asked for permission and many were not paid back fairly for the items. 

The reason that was given by the government was that they were trying to protect Canada during the war, but the real reason was to do with racism and profit. This data that was being collected was their way of trying to legitimise the choices that were being made but this was just about taken things away from people because of their race.

In many of these documents including the 1943 list of properties sold, the owners of these properties and personal belongings were often labeled as Japanese, their names were hardly mentioned. These families were not considered in the selling of their properties, the listings where distinguished by their lot number and price. Many of these houses were listed at a significantly lower cost than they should have been, including one in Vancouver that was listed at $1,500, when it was worth must more than that. This shows how the government treated these people, and how little they thought of them. 

This database focuses on the profits the government made and the numbers aspect of it all. The people that were affected and the harm that these actions caused are not mentioned in any of the documents. The way this database was set up shows how little the government cared about these people and that their focus was on the money and the control that they would gain from this injustice. 

The *Landscape of Injustice Archives* has been trying to fix some of the harm by adding some context to the document such as personal stories and maps from the victims or family of the victims. One of the maps shows were the Japanese Canadians used to live, how many homes were taken and how many lives were uprooted. This Map is called the **Map of Dispossession**. These additions help us get a better grasp at the number of lives that were affected. 

This updated version contains the side of the story the government tried to silence. This version invites people to think more critically about the event. This is a good start, but the archives could still do even more including interviews with the families to help the reader connect even more with these people. 

We are still dealing with similar problems today. We are constantly being watched and tracked. The government can use this data to control people. We must learn from our history and reading the *Landscapes of Injustice* archives shows us how the government controls the data and can write their own narrative with the information they choose to document  

Data is always controlled by someone; it will be skewed no matter what. Sometimes it is minimal interference but in this case, it is apparent that the government is only collecting the data that didn’t directly implicate them. 

The *Landscapes of Injustice* dataset shows how injustice can be implemented not just by physical force but also through paperwork and data. By representing people as numbers on a list, the government was able to disguise the harm that was caused. The revised archives are trying to show the full extent of damage that was caused by showing the human side of the story. This shows us how important data can be and how important it is to collect everything.
