### Introduction

"The Anti Eviction Mapping Project" *add hyperlink here* is a digital project that can use the framework provided by Temi Odumosu in the article "The Crying Child: On Colonial Archives, Digitization, and Ethics of Care in the Cultural Commons", drawing from the ideas that focus on the implementation of the ethics of care which aim to contextualize in a way that fosters compassion and is essential to marginalized groups, particularly in digital archiving, storytelling, and data collection and visualization practices. An additional framework of intersectionality explained in the article "Mapping the Margins: Intersectionality, Identity Politics, and Violence Against Women of Colour" written by Kimberlé Williams Crenshaw plays a key role as well in the understanding of the impact of this digital project on its ability to illuminate the overlap between multiple categories of identity, specifically in the context of residency systems working around or against these communities.

### Data Activism

This project particularly represents differences of race, gender, ethnicity, and class, making it a depiction of intersectionality. This project functions as a response to crisises of displacement due to patterns of housing  injustice and aims to provide support and resources to those in situations at risk, as well as to bring attention to these issues of gentrification.

“The Anti-Eviction Mapping Project” is a form of data activism because it creates methods of data storytelling such as data visualizations, oral histories, and zines to use the collected eviction data to strategically challenge against the hegemonies of power such as real-estate corporations, landlords, and policy makers that are in positions to inflict and inflate the struggles of housing injustice the and protect these affected tenant communities. 

For instance, the project includes multiple interactive maps functioning as data visualizations that depict areas of eviction and displacement. Most of these maps also include details like whether the building or area was owned by a corporation, real estate trusts, the public, or an individual. With this information, a correlation can be made between the number of evictions pinpointed on the map and the proprietor in power that enforced the eviction. Returning to the framework of intersectionality, users can further find patterns between overlapping groups of minority in an area, for example, a high density of lower class black women. In doing so, the affected communities are given greater context from the included data. Using this framework prevents the simplification of the meaning behind the quantitative data used to build these maps. Instead of reinforcing these intersectional categories, the project tracks these trends of displacement and reframes it toward accountability. Using this framework, the imbalance of power is put on even greater display between these marginalized groups at the mercy of their landlords and their ability to displace them, regardless of if these landlords are corporations or individuals.  This power imbalance can be so severe that the eviction of tenants of the same community repeatedly can result in the displacement of larger intersectional communities connected through economic class status, racial gender, and ethnic identities, from an area, with little no control over the outcome themselves. They live in fear of unaffordable rents, loss of culture and community as a result of displacement, racist policing, redlining, and eviction as a culmination of these factors. With this in mind “The Anti-Eviction Mapping Project” does exactly what its name suggests; it acts as a tool for these targeted communities and individuals to reclaim their histories of housing injustices and gain greater control over what the data represents. The project helps facilitate a future where tenants no longer live with these fears due to the imbalance of power and intersectional discrimination.

### The Argument

The grander argument of this digital project in short is that eviction is systemic, and is often profit-driven and racialized instead of being the result of individual failure. Displacement is eviction on a larger scale that impacts more than just the individual, and is a structural phenomenon rooted in political economy, racialized developments, and class inequality. “The Anti-Eviction Mapping Project” does so through a show versus tell method in a highly effective way which is emphasized through the design of the interface.

Through this project, it is repeatedly proven that data is not neutral. When accompanied by the correct corresponding context and given life through an interface like the one of this project, meaning is able to be produced in ways that may otherwise be overlooked. This project does not support the preexisting narratives of eviction being isolated legal events, rather it situates these events within broader historical and economic processes like gentrification, housing speculation, and tech-driven urban change. Something these data visualizations are able to argue and communicate is the correlation between systemic powers that pressure eviction with gentrification hotspots, bringing further light to how eviction and displacement have hardly ever been independent events. “The Anti-Eviction Mapping Project” uses the flexibility of using a variety of data visualizations and storytelling methods to demonstrate that evictions cluster in economically vulnerable and often historically marginalized neighbourhoods. These methods of interpreting and presenting datasets collected from the displaced communities link these eviction patterns to local laws and real estate practices like the Ellis Act and rent control rollbacks. The interface, just like the issue at hand, invites users to explore and engage with the content instead of acting as a passive viewer. The project being composed of components that are interactive from having tools for filtering and searching through the organization’s site makes the navigation more accessible. It is also more accessible because the data representations like the use of interactive maps, timeline sliders, and zines show how the changes in eviction filings over time and space such as zip codes, neighborhoods, jurisdiction, and regional boundaries in a humanly processable way. The interface of this project allows viewers to go beyond simply seeing abstract numbers by setting the stage for them to understand eviction and displacement within marginalized communities as part of a network of socioeconomic processes. It changes the numeric data into an argument about who eviction affects and how these pressures are distributed.
