## **Quantified Self-Data Project**

[View in my portfolio](https://ellaworth.xyz/selfdata/)

![Quantified Self-Data Image](images/ella-fig1.webp.webp)

After spending a week learning about my commodified data on Instagram, I’ve never felt more seen - and more ignored. For my creative project, I decided to take screenshots of every ad I was shown on Instagram over the period of a week. I took all of these ads and made them into one big collage in the form of a self-portrait, which in many ways represents who I am from the perspective of Instagram. This jumble of ads represents what I am to social platforms like Instagram and its parent company, Meta. From their perspective, I am a source of data before I am a human. My humanity and identity are wrapped up in calculated information about who they think I am and what they think I want. My clicks and prolonged glances are used to piece together my identity. The resulting advertisements they show me are an absolute quantification of what they value as important about me - how I can best engage with their platform and make them money.

In collecting my targeted advertisements, I found several interesting patterns. Of course, some ads were clearly targeted towards my demographic, such as ads searching for “Women born from 200-2006” or “Women under 25 with Anxiety”. There were also some general ads that are probably targeted to a mass audience, for example, ads for Goldfish or Starbucks. The rest of the ads, however, told me what Instagram thinks I want specifically as a result of my online behaviour. Beyond the demographic facts about me, who does Instagram think I am?

## Analyzing Advertisement Patterns

I found that my online behaviour and purchasing patterns directly influenced some of the advertising I was shown. For example, after buying fake nails, I started to get ads from Glamnetic, a popular nail company. Certain skincare brands that I use, I would see ads for. I also follow a lot of tattoo accounts and independent artists. I noticed ads for local tattoo artists and shops, as well as new artists following the style of some of the people that I follow. However, many of my ads were a little less specific to me. I got a significant number of ads for pet toys (I don’t own a pet), sports gear (i’m the least athletically inclined person in the world), and expensive travel opportunities (I would think my algorithm knows i’m not flying private to stay at the four seasons). 

Also concerning, was that I received a number of ads for Ozempic-like weight loss drugs. As someone who does not have a medical need for a drug like this, I found it interesting that Instagram would still advertise this to me, a young, thin woman. in good health (all of which can be accessible information for advertisers). I spoke to some of my friends about this who are in a similar demographic and learned they also frequently saw ads like this. I quickly realized that these companies are targeting young women on social media, many of whom are insecure about their bodies, in the digital space where the most insecurity occurs. This is perhaps good marketing but grossly unethical and medically irresponsible, saying a lot about what the priorities of these advertisers are. 

From what I understand about how Instagram collects information, I was rather surprised at the number of products they were pushing that were clearly out of character and out of line with my online behaviour. One theory I have is that it may have had to do with how I was interacting with Instagram during that time, an unintended bias I may have caused. I was screenshotting or interacting with every ad that I saw, so perhaps I spent more time looking at an ad for pet food, leading Instagram to think it was something I was interested in. 

## Instagram/Meta Terms and Conditions Breakdown

To further understand exactly how and why I was seeing the ads that I was, I did a thorough investigation of Instagram’s Terms & Conditions [^1], as well as their Privacy Policy [^2]. Some key things that I learned include the fact that they have access to information involved in any app that you use that Meta has the information rights to - games, ads, etc. As one of the biggest tech giants in the world, the scope of apps that Meta owns is massive, which means the sources they have to collect data are just as big. They can access camera rolls, contacts, and even tracked location spotting. It's mentioned that they can also get information about consumers’ technology from “third-party industry peers” [^3]. This phrase makes it look more legitimate than it is, though; these "industry peers" can include law enforcement, academic institutions, advertising companies, or even charities and other NGOs (Non-governmental organizations), to name a few [^4]. As quoted in their privacy policy, “We use information we have, information from researchers and datasets from publicly available sources, professional groups and non-profit groups to conduct and support research.”. [^5]

In the course reading/podcast “Fresh Air”, Geoffrey Fowler further explains the business model of social media data [^6]. He shares that the policies are technically correct. Platforms don’t sell data because it’s more profitable for them to keep it to themselves. However, what they don’t talk about is trading/sharing data. The way its phrased is misleading because Instagram does share information with outside advertisers to a certain degree. It’s still essentially selling data, but any data sold is used for advertising within Instagram’s own platform. 

When looking to the future, I wonder how the digital quantification of myself might look different over time. In “The Policy of Platforms” by Tarleton Gillespie [^7], Gillespie addresses how policy and privacy laws might affect the performance of these algorithms. Currently, information giants like Meta are very interested in getting involved with government and policymakers. This is in an attempt to preserve their business model in an era where many politicians want to introduce more privacy protections for citizens. Aggressive lobbyists have been hired by these giant companies to protect their current access to private information. Gillespie also notes the current state of privacy in California. Compared to every other state in the U.S, California has the most protective privacy laws. It's pretty easy to piece together why. Tech development happens on the biggest scale in California, specifically in Silicon Valley and San Francisco. The people who live there often work in tech and understand firsthand the privacy risks associated with the technology that they themselves are developing. I think this makes a statement that the way data is stored and shared is risky and is something we should protect.

This project revealed that Instagram’s algorithmic quantification of myself is both scarily accurate and, in many ways, quite off-base. While some of my advertisements reflected my real-life interests, some of them were clearly disconnected from my identity. This inconsistency suggests that while the data collection is extensive, the advertisers don’t always use it successfully. Even more troubling, it shows how Instagram as a platform can exploit the data and the vulnerabilities of people, pushing harmful messages and products like weight-loss drugs for consumers who don’t need them, primarily young women. 

## Guise of Transparency

My investigation into Meta’s policies confirmed what critics like Fowler and Gillespoe have argued in our course texts. Social media companies operate under the guise of transparency while carefully hiding the ways in which they can access all aspects of your web presence, even outside of the use of their app. Surveillance capitalism has become essential to these companies and is the core of their business model. But what if it weren’t? If companies couldn’t access all of the information about us that they can currently, what would that do to the world of social media advertising/monetization? We can’t fully know, but the fact that tech giants are fighting so hard against privacy regulation can give us a clue as to how it might negatively affect their profits.

Ultimately, this project helped me observe the knowledge I had about social media algorithms and see how it played out for me specifically. In this, I learned that Instagram isn’t a true reflection of my identity but a commodified reflection. My identity is reduced to data points that advertisers don’t even care about being hyper-specific about. My data is not primarily collected to enhance my experience but to first and foremost serve corporate interests. When looking at the final product of the collage I created, I didn’t see just products, but a twisted version of who I am, which is why I chose to demonstrate the collage as a self-portrait, even replacing my facial features with ads targeting those very features. Instead of my nose, it’s an ad for pore cleansing. My eyes and mouth don’t belong to me, but a version of who I could be if I buy and engage with the advertised products. 

## Path Forward

Moving forward, my question is no longer how algorithms define me but how I feel about their existence continuing to go unchecked. Stronger policies protecting citizens in California are offering a glimpse into what further privacy restrictions could look like, but real change would likely require more self-advocacy from users to hold the platforms accountable. Until this happens, we are no more than monetary objects. Hopefully, the future of social media can exist for democratic and entertainment purposes above all else and respect individual privacy.
